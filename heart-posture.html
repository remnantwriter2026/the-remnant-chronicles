Heart Posture

What's Behind What You Do

By Jimey Grant Lafayette, Louisiana Devotional Chronicle

Everybody wants to know what love really looks like. We see it, we feel it, but it's hard to explain in a way that actually works day to day. Jesus made it simple. He said it plain: "Love your neighbor as yourself." Then he added something bigger — "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets." That means every command, every story, every prophet in the Bible connects back to love. Love isn't just something you do. It's a fruit of the Spirit, which means it grows from something happening inside you first. In Luke 6:45, Jesus said it this way: "For of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh." What's inside you eventually comes out. Did you know that the word "heart" is mentioned over 700 times in the Bible? Every time, it's pointing to the same idea: it's not just what you do that matters to God. It's what's behind what you do. This piece is about that — heart posture — and why it shows up again and again in the lives of the men and women God used.

In fact, love itself is proof of it. Love is a confirmation of the transformational act of salvation — it starts on the inside and then manifests on the outside in how we live, act, and treat others.

Consider Paul. Before he was an apostle, he was a man who hunted down Christians with hatred in his chest. That's not a minor character flaw — that's murder, born from a heart completely bent the wrong way. Yet Scripture calls this kind of change "the renewing of the mind." In the Bible, heart and mind are often the same thing. God didn't just change Paul's behavior. He changed his posture.

Scripture gives two more witnesses to how this works — one calling fire down, one refusing to.

In 1 Kings 18:30-38, Elijah stands before Israel and prays for fire from heaven. But listen to his motive: he doesn't ask for spectacle. He asks that the people would know God is Lord, and that their hearts would turn back to Him. The fire falls because the posture behind the prayer was aligned with God's own heart.

In Luke 9:51-55, James and John ask Jesus the same kind of thing — call down fire, this time on a village that rejected them. Same request. Different heart. Theirs was born of offense, not love. Jesus rebukes them on the spot.

Same power requested. Opposite posture behind it. Only one got answered.

Then there's Aquila and Priscilla — a married couple who show us what heart posture looks like when it's lived out day to day, not just in a single dramatic moment. In Romans 16, Paul says they "risked their own necks" for his life. Think about what that actually means. Paul was a marked man — hated and hunted, eventually put to death for the gospel he preached. To risk your neck for someone like that means putting your own life on the line, knowing full well what could happen to you if you were caught helping him. Aquila and Priscilla didn't do that because Paul was famous or because they'd get something out of it. They did it because their hearts were already postured toward love before the danger ever showed up. That's the difference between a heart that has to talk itself into sacrifice and a heart that's already bent that direction, ready the moment it's needed.

And it didn't stop there. They opened their own home so the church could meet, worship, and grow. No stage, no title, no platform — just two people whose hearts were positioned to give whatever they had. That's the early church in a nutshell: not perfect people with perfect resources, but ordinary people with rightly postured hearts, doing whatever love required.

That's the pattern running through every story in this piece — from Paul to Elijah to James and John to Aquila and Priscilla. It was never about what was said or done on the outside. It was about what was true on the inside first. So ask yourself, honestly: what's the posture of your heart right now? Not what you're doing for others, but what's moving in you while you do it. Because love — real love — isn't a performance you put on when people are watching. It's a posture your heart holds even when no one's looking, even when nothing's in it for you. Paul had it. Elijah had it. Aquila and Priscilla had it. The only question left is whether you do.

And the greatest witness of all is Jesus Himself. "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son" — the cross wasn't forced on Him. It was the ultimate proof of heart posture, love poured out before it was ever earned. Every witness in this piece points back to that one.
