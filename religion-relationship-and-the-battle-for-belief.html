Religion, Relationship, and the Battle for Belief

“See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition and the elemental spiritual forces of this world rather than on Christ.” — Colossians 2:8

By Jimey Grant Lafayette, Louisiana Second Watch

As a student of religion and the social sciences, including psychology and sociology, I see the warning in 1 Timothy 4:1 and the call to transformation in Romans 12:2 unfolding around us. Paul warns that some will turn away from the faith and follow deceiving spirits and teachings that lead them astray. He also urges believers not to conform to the patterns of this world, but to be transformed by the renewing of their minds. Together, these passages call us to discernment. Our beliefs can be shaped by the messages we encounter, the systems around us, and the people we trust to interpret events and faith. As we consider how religion, society, repetition, and media can influence what people believe, we must ask whether those influences draw us closer to Christ or lead us away from the truth. This makes it essential to distinguish outward religious practice from a personal relationship with Christ.

To understand how religion can influence the way people think and live, it helps to examine the systems and processes that shape belief. The following concepts—religion, sociology, indoctrination, programming, learning, and repetition—offer a framework for considering how beliefs take root and affect individuals and communities.

Religion is commonly understood as an organized system of beliefs, practices, and traditions related to the sacred or divine. It gives people shared symbols and teachings through which they express their faith, make sense of life, and connect with a community. In society, these shared beliefs and practices can foster a sense of belonging and contribute to social order.

Sociology is the scientific study of society, human behavior, and the systems and structures that shape everyday life. Indoctrination is the process of teaching a person or group to accept a particular set of beliefs, ideas, or values. Programming refers to the ways people use their minds to think through problems, remember and organize information, and turn ideas into instructions they can understand. Learning is the process of acquiring knowledge, behaviors, skills, values, or attitudes through experience, study, or instruction.

Repetition is one of the ways the brain learns and reinforces ideas. When people accept ideas without discernment, repetition—along with religious language, psychological influence, and suggestion—can shape what they believe. These influences can reach us through many channels, including television and cellphones. This is why Scripture urges us in 1 John 4:1 not to believe every spirit, but to test them.

Psalm 115:16 says, “The highest heavens belong to the Lord, but the earth he has given to mankind.” Human influence is reflected in the world around us—in our psychologies, philosophies, ideologies, customs, and beliefs.

We have also to take into account that such institutions that control the media may also be familiar with the Bible and other religious texts. Knowing this, they understand that influencing the media means influencing the narrative.

Consider the pattern: preachers, teachers, and prophets watch politics and the news, compare what they see with Scripture, and declare it prophecy. Now let’s reverse the roles for a moment. Imagine thinking like an elite group seeking to influence people’s religious beliefs through the media. They know that some people turn to the news to learn what is happening around the world and then look to the Bible to interpret those events. If people are already taught to watch for wars, rumors of wars, and disease outbreaks, a carefully shaped narrative could lead them to believe they are witnessing a particular biblical prophecy—even when that conclusion may be wrong or based on a false-flag narrative. People’s emotions become caught up in the speculation, while those shaping the narrative watch their reactions.

That is why a personal relationship with Christ matters so deeply: it points us beyond outward religion and toward a life rooted in Him. In Colossians 2:6–7, Paul urges believers to continue living in Christ, “rooted and built up in him” and strengthened in the faith. He also reminds them that they are made complete in Christ (Colossians 2:9–10). Our confidence, then, rests not in impressing others or meeting every human expectation, but in belonging to Jesus and continuing to walk with Him. This chapter calls us to more than religious language or familiar practices; it describes a living trust in Christ—a relationship that shapes our convictions, choices, and character. Religious traditions may have a place in our lives, but they can never replace knowing Christ personally and growing in Him.
